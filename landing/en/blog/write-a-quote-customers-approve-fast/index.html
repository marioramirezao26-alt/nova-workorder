How to write a quote your customers approve fast

October 29, 2026 · 4 min read · By the NOVAWORKORDER team

A quote that sits for days without an answer almost always has one of four problems: it arrived late, it's hard to understand, it's missing what the customer needs to decide, or it's a hassle to approve. All four can be fixed, and none of them require lowering your price.

This guide is for service businesses (HVAC, refrigeration, plumbing, electrical, maintenance), but most of the advice works for any kind of service.

1. Send it fast, while the problem is fresh

The best moment for a customer to say yes is right after the visit, when they've just seen the broken unit and heard your explanation. Every day that goes by, the problem feels less urgent, and the temptation to get another quote grows.

Set yourself a rule: quotes for simple jobs go out the same day. To make that possible, keep reference prices for your most common services and parts, so you don't start every quote from scratch.

2. Make it clear without a phone call

The customer should be able to read it alone and know exactly what they're getting. For that:

  • A title that names the job: "Leak repair and refrigerant recharge, office 3 AC unit". Not "Quote #0231".
  • One line per item, with quantity, unit price and total. "Labor and materials" on a single line breeds distrust.
  • The customer's words, not the technician's. If you use a technical term, explain it briefly: "capacitor (the part that helps the motor start)".
  • Clear sales tax: show whether it's included or added, and how much it is.
  • The total, big and easy to find.

3. Say what's included and what isn't

Most misunderstandings show up later, when the customer thought something was included. Prevent them by writing down:

  • What's included: "includes removal, cleaning, the part and a test run".
  • What's not included: "doesn't include scaffolding or drywall repair".
  • Warranty: how long and on what (the part, the labor or both).
  • Timing: when you can do it and how long it takes.

4. Give options, but only a few

When a customer sees one price, the question in their head is "yes or no?". When they see two or three options, the question becomes "which one?". For example: repair the current unit, repair it and add a maintenance plan, or replace it. More than three options is confusing; two or three is enough.

Each option should say clearly what makes it different, so the customer doesn't have to guess.

5. Show why the work is needed

A photo of the dirty coil, the oily line or the burnt wire is more convincing than any explanation. If you took photos during the visit, mention them or attach them. And if you do maintenance visits, the readings you wrote down last time are your best argument: "the compressor is drawing more current than at the last visit".

6. Give it an expiration date

"Prices valid for 15 days" protects your margin if parts costs change, and gives the customer a fair reason to decide. Make it a real date, not invented pressure.

7. Make approving easy

If the customer has to print, sign, scan and send it back, it'll take a while. The easiest way is letting them say yes from their phone, right in the message where they got the quote. And spell out what happens next: "once you approve, we'll text you to schedule the visit".

If you ask for a deposit, say so in the quote: how much, how it's paid and when. A surprise at the end slows everything down.

8. Follow up without pushing

Many customers don't answer because they forgot, not because they're saying no. A short message two or three days later works: "Hi, did you get a chance to look at the quote for the office 3 AC? Happy to answer any questions". If the customer declines, ask why: sometimes it's the price, but often it's the timing or something that wasn't clear.

9. Turn the approved quote into a work order

The last common mistake is getting the approval and then forgetting about it. When the customer says yes, create the work order with exactly what was quoted and assign it. That way the technician knows what was sold and the customer gets what they approved. If your work orders still live on paper, start with how to organize work orders without paper.

A structure you can copy

  1. Job title and unit or area.
  2. A short diagnosis (two lines), with a photo if you have one.
  3. Line items: description, quantity, unit price, total.
  4. Subtotal, sales tax and total.
  5. What's included, what's not and the warranty.
  6. Timing and payment terms (deposit if any).
  7. Expiration date.
  8. How to approve.
